Costa Rica's real estate market is genuinely favorable for foreign buyers in most respects. The country has a stable legal system, a public property registry, and no restrictions on foreign ownership of most real estate. The fundamentals are sound.

The problems that do occur are structural. They are not random — they cluster around specific failure points that repeat across transactions. Having been through the development and acquisition process multiple times in this market, I can tell you exactly what those failure points are.

This article covers the five risks that kill or complicate Costa Rica real estate transactions, and explains the structural choices we made at Black Coast Estates specifically to eliminate them.

## Risk 1: Maritime Zone Misclassification

**What it is:** Costa Rica's Maritime Zone Law (Ley de la Zona Marítimo Terrestre, No. 6043) divides the coastal zone into two bands: a 50-meter public zone (zona pública) measured from the average high tide line, and a 150-meter restricted zone (zona restringida) beyond that. Properties in the public zone cannot be privately owned. Properties in the restricted zone can only be held under government concession — a lease from the local municipality — not in fee simple ownership.

**How buyers get hurt:** Properties are sometimes marketed as "beachfront" without clear disclosure of which zone they occupy. A buyer who purchases what they believe is a fee simple property discovers post-closing that they actually hold a concession with a finite term, renewal uncertainty, and restrictions on development and sale that do not apply to titled fee simple land.

Concession properties are not worthless — they can be valuable and well-structured — but they have fundamentally different legal characteristics than titled land, and a buyer who paid fee-simple prices for a concession asset has a problem.

**How to verify:** Request the property's folio real (title registration number) and the exact coordinates. Have your attorney confirm whether the property falls within the Maritime Zone using the Catastro Nacional maps. If any portion of the property is within 200 meters of the high tide line, require explicit documentation of the zoning classification for every parcel involved.

## Risk 2: Boundary and Survey Inconsistencies

**What it is:** Costa Rica's property registry system uses cadastral surveys (planos catastrados) to define property boundaries. These surveys are not always recent, not always consistent with physical improvements on the ground, and not always correctly matched to the title registration.

In rural coastal areas — exactly the markets most attractive to international buyers — the probability of a boundary discrepancy is meaningfully higher than in urban areas. Older properties may have surveys that predate current GPS standards. Neighbors may have encroached on the property over time. Easements may not be reflected in the registered survey.

**How buyers get hurt:** A buyer closes on a property. They commission a new survey to begin construction. The new survey shows the property is smaller than the registered description, or that a neighboring structure encroaches on their land, or that an access road they assumed was theirs is actually on a third-party easement. These disputes are resolvable but they are expensive, time-consuming, and sometimes result in a property that is functionally different from what was purchased.

**How to verify:** Commission an independent survey by a licensed Costa Rican topographer (topógrafo) before closing, comparing the physical boundaries against the registered plano catastrado. This costs $500 to $1,500 and is money that pays for itself in roughly 20% of coastal property transactions.

**Our structure:** Our development has current, GPS-calibrated surveys on all parcels, reviewed and certified by an independent topographer. The survey documentation is part of the due diligence package provided to every buyer.

## Risk 3: HOA and Community Structure Liability

**What it is:** Residential developments in Costa Rica frequently operate under some form of homeowner association structure. In newer developments, this is often a filial association (asociación de condóminos) or a private road and services agreement among lot owners. The quality of these structures varies enormously.

**How buyers get hurt:** A buyer purchases in a development with an HOA structure. Post-closing, they discover that: the HOA fees are significantly higher than disclosed; the reserve fund is depleted and a special assessment is coming; the private road that provides access has a maintenance dispute between the HOA and adjacent landowners; or the association is controlled by a small group of owners who make decisions that do not reflect the majority's interests.

The most egregious version of this risk is the "controlled HOA" structure where the developer retains majority control of the HOA after sales begin, allowing them to charge management fees that benefit their affiliated companies indefinitely.

**How to verify:** Request the HOA governing documents before signing. Specifically: the articles of association, the fee schedule for the current year and the prior 3 years, the reserve fund balance, and the minutes from the last 3 annual meetings. If any of these documents are not available or the seller declines to provide them, that is a significant red flag.

**Our structure:** The Black Coast Estates co-ownership agreement is governed by a co-owner committee with equal voting rights among all co-owners. No single party — including the developer — holds a majority. Management fees are fixed at a disclosed rate with annual adjustment tied to the Costa Rican consumer price index. The financial statements are provided to co-owners annually.

## Risk 4: Construction Quality in a Foreign Jurisdiction

**What it is:** Building standards in Costa Rica are governed by CFIA (Colegio Federado de Ingenieros y de Arquitectos) and the National Building Code. These standards are real and enforceable, but the enforcement mechanism — which depends on periodic inspector visits and final permit sign-off — is less rigorous than what buyers from the US or Europe typically expect.

The result is that construction quality in Costa Rica can vary dramatically between developers. Buyers from markets where building inspections are thorough and continuous sometimes underestimate this variance.

Specific risk factors: seismic zone requirements (Guanacaste is in a moderate seismic zone; foundations and structural connections must be engineered accordingly), tropical humidity resistance in structural and finishing materials, drainage and waterproofing in coastal environments, and electrical work performed to North American standards versus local practice.

**How buyers get hurt:** They purchase a property with cosmetically impressive finishes that covers structural or systems deficiencies. Problems emerge in year two or three: foundation cracking, water intrusion, electrical issues, or a roof that fails in the first serious rainy season.

**How to verify:** For resale properties, commission an independent structural inspection by a Costa Rica CFIA-certified engineer. For new construction, request the engineering permits, the structural plans stamped by a CFIA engineer, and the construction permits issued by the local municipality. If a developer cannot produce these documents, the building may have been constructed without proper permits — a risk that can complicate future sale or renovation.

## Risk 5: Seller Entity and Authorization Complexity

**What it is:** Many Costa Rica properties are held by a Sociedad Anónima (S.A.) or Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada (S.R.L.) — a corporate entity rather than an individual. This is standard practice and has tax and liability advantages for owners. It also creates a specific acquisition risk that buyers often miss.

When you purchase a property from a corporate entity, you are relying on the representations of whoever is executing the purchase agreement to have actual authority to sell. A corporation can only sell property through its authorized representatives, whose authority is defined in the corporate charter (escritura social) and must be current and unrevoked.

**How buyers get hurt:** A transaction closes. Post-closing, a director of the selling corporation — or a minority shareholder who was not party to the sale — challenges the authority of the person who signed. In the clearest version of this risk, a married couple owns a property through an S.A., they separate, and one spouse sells the property without the other's knowledge or consent. The buyer has a deed that may be challenged.

This risk also applies at the corporate structure level: if the selling entity has undisclosed liabilities, tax debts, or pending legal claims, those may attach to the property even after transfer to a new owner.

**How to verify:** Request and review the complete corporate documentation of the selling entity: escritura social, current accionista (shareholder) registry, and evidence that there are no pending tax obligations or liens against the corporation. Your attorney should also confirm that the individual signing on behalf of the corporation has current, unrevoked authority to do so.
