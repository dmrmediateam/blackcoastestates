Every month I have a version of the same conversation. A buyer is genuinely interested in co-ownership. They have read the numbers, they understand the model, and they are intellectually convinced. Then someone — a parent, a financial advisor, a friend who bought a timeshare in 1997 — says "be careful with those fractional deals" and the interest cools.

The skepticism is understandable. The timeshare industry spent decades selling "fractional vacation ownership" to people and delivering something that looked nothing like ownership. The residue of that industry is a justified suspicion that needs to be addressed directly.

This article draws the line precisely.

## What a Timeshare Actually Is

A timeshare is a usage right, not an ownership interest. When you buy a timeshare, you are purchasing the contractual right to use a specific unit (or a unit in a defined resort network) for a specific period each year — typically one week.

**You do not own anything.** There is no deed reflecting your name as a property owner. There is no equity in the underlying asset. When the resort chain decides to renovate, rebrand, or close the property, you have no say and no claim on the resulting value.

**The liability is perpetual.** Most timeshares come with annual maintenance fees — typically $1,000 to $2,000 per year and rising — that continue regardless of whether you use the property. These fees are contractual obligations that transfer with the timeshare if you attempt to sell it (and the resale market for timeshares is, functionally, near zero for most products).

**The "investment" framing is fiction.** Timeshare companies have been successfully sued in multiple jurisdictions for misrepresenting usage rights as investments. The settlements are a matter of public record. A timeshare does not appreciate. It does not generate rental income for you. It does not build equity.

The only beneficiary of a timeshare transaction over a long hold is the resort operator, who collects development profit on the sale and perpetual fee income thereafter.

## What Equity Co-Ownership Actually Is

Equity co-ownership — structured correctly — is the legal and economic opposite of a timeshare.

**You own a deeded fractional interest in the property.** Your name, along with the names of your co-owners, appears on the title recorded with the relevant public registry. In Costa Rica, this registration is with the Registro Nacional and is publicly verifiable. You own real property.

**Your interest has economic value independent of usage.** When the property appreciates, your fractional interest appreciates proportionally. When the property generates rental income, you receive your proportional share. When the property is eventually sold, you receive your proportional proceeds. These are the economic rights of an owner, not a renter with a long lease.

**The structure is governed by a co-ownership agreement, not resort management terms.** A properly structured co-ownership agreement specifies: how usage weeks are allocated among co-owners, how capital decisions (major repairs, improvements, eventual sale) are made, what happens if one co-owner wants to exit, and how the property is managed. This is a contract between co-owners protecting their shared interest, not a contract between a buyer and a resort operator protecting the operator's interest.

**You can sell your interest.** A fractional interest in a property with a clean title and a well-drafted co-ownership agreement is a saleable asset. You can transfer it to a buyer, include it in your estate, or sell it through a broker. The market for fractional interests in well-located luxury properties is small but real. The market for timeshares is functionally zero.

## The Four Questions That Separate Real Co-Ownership from Dressed-Up Timeshares

When evaluating any fractional or co-ownership offering, ask these four questions. The answers tell you immediately which category you are dealing with.

**1. Is there a deed?**
Real co-ownership: Yes. Your fractional interest is recorded as a property interest in the relevant public registry. You can request a certified copy of your title registration.
Timeshare: No. The documentation you receive is a contract for usage rights, not a deed.

**2. Do you receive rental income?**
Real co-ownership: Yes. When the property is rented during periods you are not in residence, the rental income (net of management fees) is distributed to co-owners in proportion to their ownership percentage.
Timeshare: No. Rental income from your unused weeks goes to the resort operator. In some cases, you can "bank" unused weeks within a points system, but you receive no economic benefit from the rental of the unit during your period.

**3. Do you participate in appreciation?**
Real co-ownership: Yes. When the property is sold — whether in 5 years or 20 — you receive your proportional share of the sale proceeds. If the property has appreciated, you capture that gain.
Timeshare: No. The appreciation in the underlying real estate belongs entirely to the resort operator. Your usage right does not appreciate; it depreciates. The resale value of most timeshares is less than the original purchase price — often significantly less.

**4. What are the ongoing fees, and what are they for?**
Real co-ownership: A HOA-style fee covering property maintenance, insurance, and property management — split proportionally among co-owners. These fees should be predictable, disclosed at purchase, and governed by the co-ownership agreement.
Timeshare: An open-ended maintenance fee set and adjusted unilaterally by the resort operator. There is no contractual cap. These fees have increased an average of 5% to 8% annually across major US timeshare operators over the past decade, with no meaningful recourse for owners who object.

## Why This Matters for a Costa Rica Investment Specifically

Costa Rica's real estate market has attracted its share of both legitimate fractional developers and outright fraudulent operators over the past 20 years. The fraudulent operators have consistently dressed timeshare-style products in co-ownership language.

The structural protections above are more important here than in a US domestic transaction, because the legal enforcement mechanisms for a defrauded foreign buyer in Costa Rica are slower and more expensive than they would be domestically.

Before signing any fractional or co-ownership agreement in Costa Rica:

- Verify the title registration exists and matches what you are being sold
- Have the co-ownership agreement reviewed by an independent Costa Rican attorney (not the developer's attorney)
- Confirm the property LLC structure if one is used, and verify that your ownership interest in the LLC reflects your stated fractional percentage
- Request the full financial disclosure for the property management fee structure and rental income distribution

These are not adversarial requests. A legitimate co-ownership developer will have this documentation ready and will provide it without friction. That readiness is itself a signal.
