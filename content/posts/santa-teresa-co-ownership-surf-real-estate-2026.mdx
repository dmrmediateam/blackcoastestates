Santa Teresa has a supply problem. Not in the "there's nothing to buy" sense — in the "the people who love it most can no longer afford to buy it" sense.

I've watched this pattern play out in real estate markets across the Western Hemisphere. A place gets discovered by adventurous early adopters. Word spreads. Prices rise. The early adopters who built the culture get priced out. The character that attracted the prices starts to dilute.

Santa Teresa is mid-cycle in that story. And co-ownership is the mechanism that's letting a second wave of buyers participate without the full capital exposure that the current market demands.

## How Santa Teresa Got Here

Ten years ago, Santa Teresa was a dirt-road surf town at the southern tip of the Nicoya Peninsula. The access was genuinely difficult — a ferry from Puntarenas, then a long, rough drive, or a small plane to the Tambor airport. That difficulty was a filter. The people who made the trip were surfers, artists, and travelers who valued what the difficulty protected.

Then the road improved. The flights became easier. Instagram found the beach. By 2022, Santa Teresa was appearing on "World's Best Beach Towns" lists in Travel + Leisure and Condé Nast Traveler. By 2024, the major surf media was covering real estate there alongside the waves.

The median sale price for a luxury property in the Playa Carmen-Santa Teresa corridor now sits at $1.9M. Prime oceanfront has traded at $4M to $6M. Branded residences from international developers are in planning.

The culture that created the demand is still partially there. But it is competing with boutique hotels, celebrity-adjacent villas, and a tourist infrastructure that increasingly looks like Tulum circa 2019.

## Why Co-Ownership Works Specifically Here

The co-ownership model — properly structured equity co-ownership, not timeshare — addresses a specific problem in the Santa Teresa market.

The problem: the buyer who wants to spend four to eight weeks per year in Santa Teresa doesn't need a $2M asset. They need $2M worth of Santa Teresa for four to eight weeks. The traditional ownership model forces a complete mismatch between capital deployed and value consumed.

A co-ownership structure with 4 to 8 equal shares solves this directly. Each co-owner pays $250K to $500K for proportional ownership of a $2M asset. Each receives a deeded fractional interest in the property — not a usage right, not a membership, but actual equity. Each receives proportional rental income when they are not in residence. Each participates in appreciation when the property is eventually sold.

The economics change completely. A $250K entry into a Santa Teresa co-ownership structure carries the same appreciation exposure as a $2M purchase at one-eighth the capital. The rental yield — typically 8% to 12% on the full asset value — applies proportionally. You're not buying a smaller piece of the pie. You're buying a piece of the same pie.

## The Scalability Factor

Here's what I find most interesting from a developer's perspective: co-ownership scales in ways that traditional full-ownership developments do not.

A developer selling four co-ownership shares at $250K each captures $1M per property — the equivalent of a traditional sale at $1M. But the property can be priced and finished to a $1.5M to $2M standard because four buyers are underwriting the cost. The gap between capital raised and asset quality is the developer's margin, and it is structurally wider than in traditional single-buyer sales.

For buyers, this means they are consistently entering a property that is finished to a higher standard than their purchase price would imply in a traditional ownership structure. In a market like Santa Teresa, where rental income depends on delivering a genuinely premium experience, this quality gap is commercially significant.

## What the Rental Data Shows

We track vacation rental performance across Guanacaste and the Nicoya Peninsula closely. Santa Teresa's rental data is compelling and instructive.

Average nightly rates for luxury three and four-bedroom properties in the Santa Teresa corridor: $850 to $1,400. Occupancy rates for well-managed, well-photographed properties: 62% to 74% annually. Gross yield on a $1.8M property at these metrics: approximately 10% to 14%.

The rental income is real. The driver is not just the beach — it is the specific combination that Santa Teresa delivers: world-class surf, a curated food and wellness scene, a social atmosphere that attracts a demographic with disposable income and a preference for boutique over branded. That combination is not easily replicated, which is why the rental premiums persist even as prices have risen.

## The Comparison: Santa Teresa vs. Playa Negra Co-Ownership

I want to be honest about this because we operate in Playa Negra, not Santa Teresa. The comparison is worth making directly.

Santa Teresa is a more mature market with higher current prices and an established rental track record. If you can enter co-ownership there at a rational valuation, the rental income certainty is higher. The appreciation upside, however, is proportionally lower — you are buying into a market that has already had its major discovery event.

[Playa Negra](https://blackcoastestates.com) offers a different profile: lower entry prices, higher appreciation upside, and a surf destination that is at the beginning of its discovery curve rather than the middle. The rental yield fundamentals are comparable. The price appreciation trajectory is not — Playa Negra is running 6% to 9% year-over-year, Santa Teresa closer to 4% to 6%.

For a buyer building a portfolio, both have roles. For a buyer making a single allocation in Costa Rica surf real estate in 2026, the asymmetry of the Playa Negra opportunity is meaningful.

## Who the Co-Ownership Buyer Is

The buyer who chooses co-ownership over full ownership is not someone who can't afford full ownership. In many cases, they can. They choose co-ownership because they are making a portfolio decision, not a lifestyle decision.

A $250K co-ownership position in a Santa Teresa or Playa Negra property is a rational allocation for someone with $2M to $5M of investable assets who wants real estate exposure in Costa Rica without concentrating capital in a single illiquid asset. It is the same logic that drives institutional investors to use REITS rather than buying office buildings outright — diversification, liquidity optionality, and professional management.

The difference is that co-ownership in a luxury surf property delivers something a REIT cannot: four to eight weeks per year of actually being there. That experiential return does not appear in the yield calculations but it is real, and it is one of the primary reasons this model is growing.

## The Structure That Protects You

One thing I want to address directly: not all co-ownership structures are created equal. The market for fractional real estate has producers who use the language of co-ownership while delivering something closer to a usage contract with no genuine equity.

The structure that protects co-owners has four components: a deed reflecting each owner's fractional interest in the property, a professionally drafted co-ownership agreement governing usage scheduling and capital decisions, a property LLC or equivalent legal entity holding the asset in the relevant jurisdiction, and a management arrangement with clear fee disclosure and termination rights.

If a co-ownership offering cannot produce documentation on all four points before you sign anything, walk away.
