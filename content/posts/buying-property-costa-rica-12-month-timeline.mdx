I talk to buyers every week who have done the research, know the basics of Costa Rica property law, have their financing lined up, and are ready to move. They ask me how long it takes to close.

When I tell them 90 to 180 days for a clean, uncomplicated transaction — and longer if anything surfaces in due diligence — the silence on the other end of the call is always the same.

Nobody told them.

This guide is the information that most sales conversations skip over, because it is not inspiring and it does not move people toward yes. It is, however, the truth about what buying property in Costa Rica actually looks like from start to close.

## Months 1-2: Market Education and Property Identification

The first two months are rarely wasted time if you use them correctly. They should produce: a clear understanding of which corridor and price range matches your objectives, a shortlist of two to five properties, and a relationship with a buyer's advocate or sales contact who knows the local inventory.

What slows people down: spending these months looking at listings without a clear investment thesis. Costa Rica's real estate market does not have the MLS infrastructure of the US. Listings on international portals are often outdated, inconsistently priced, and sometimes already sold. The good inventory is frequently not listed publicly — it moves through broker networks and direct referrals.

**The practical approach:** Identify two or three properties you are genuinely interested in and initiate contact with the listing broker or developer. Ask directly: Is this property available? What is the realistic timeline to close? Are there any known title complications? The answers to those three questions tell you more than six months of passive searching.

## Month 2-3: Due Diligence — The Part That Actually Takes Time

Once you have identified a property and have a letter of intent or purchase agreement in principle, due diligence begins. This is where timelines start to slip.

**Title search (2 to 4 weeks for clean properties, longer if complications exist):** Costa Rica uses a public registry (Registro Nacional) where all properties are recorded. A title search must confirm: the seller has clear legal title, there are no liens or encumbrances, the property's cadastral survey matches the physical boundaries, and there are no pending legal claims.

If the property has changed hands multiple times, or if boundary surveys are outdated (common in rural coastal areas), this process extends significantly. We have seen title searches on properties with complex ownership histories run 8 to 12 weeks.

**Concession zone clarification (critical for beachfront properties):** In Costa Rica, the Maritime Zone Law establishes a 200-meter buffer from the average high tide line. The first 50 meters are public domain and cannot be privately owned. The next 150 meters are the "restricted zone" — they can be leased from the government under a concession, but not owned in fee simple.

If you are buying beachfront, you need to understand exactly which zone your property occupies, what type of concession governs it, when that concession expires, and what the renewal terms are. This is non-negotiable due diligence. Ask the question directly and get written documentation in response.

**Environmental permits:** Properties in conservation or transition zones may have restrictions on construction, tree removal, or land use changes. SETENA approval may be required for development plans. If you intend to build, remodel significantly, or develop the property, environmental permit status must be confirmed before closing.

## Month 3-4: Financing (If Applicable)

Most international buyers purchase Costa Rica real estate with cash or offshore financing. Local Costa Rican bank financing is available to foreign buyers but is structurally different from US mortgages in ways that matter:

- Interest rates are typically 7% to 10% in USD (as of 2026)
- Loan-to-value ratios are commonly 50% to 70%, not the 80% to 90% available in the US
- The approval process involves financial documentation that foreign buyers often find cumbersome: proof of income translated and apostilled, credit history verification through international bureaus, and a property appraisal by a bank-approved appraiser that may differ significantly from the agreed purchase price

If you are paying cash, this phase does not apply — but verify that your funds can be wired internationally to Costa Rica without triggering the anti-money laundering documentation requirements that some buyers are surprised by. Transfers above $10,000 USD require source-of-funds documentation at the receiving bank. Large transfers may require advance coordination with both sending and receiving institutions.

## Month 4-5: Negotiation and Purchase Agreement

The purchase-sale agreement (escritura de promesa de compraventa) is typically where the transaction is formalized. This document sets the final price, deposit structure, closing timeline, and conditions.

**Things buyers commonly miss in purchase agreements:**

- **Earnest money terms:** In Costa Rica, a typical deposit is 10% of the purchase price. Under standard terms, this deposit is non-refundable if the buyer cancels without a valid legal reason. Ensure the agreement includes specific conditions under which the deposit is refundable — title defects, permit problems, and failed financing are the standard ones.

- **Closing date flexibility:** Build in a grace period. Closing dates in Costa Rica are aspirational. The notary-attorney who handles the transaction may have scheduling conflicts, the registry may have processing delays, and last-minute documentation issues are common. A 30-day extension clause is standard practice.

- **Furniture and fixtures:** Specify in writing what is included. Costa Rican law does not have a "fixtures stay" default equivalent to US real estate custom. The high-end appliances, outdoor furniture, and artwork that made you fall in love with the property are personal property unless explicitly listed in the agreement.

## Month 5-6: Transfer Tax and Closing Costs

Costa Rica's transfer tax is 1.5% of the registered property value (not necessarily the purchase price — the registered value and sale price sometimes differ, which has its own complications). Stamp duties add approximately 0.45%.

Total closing costs typically run 3% to 4% of the purchase price when you include: transfer tax, stamp duties, notary fees (typically 1.25% under the Costa Rican fee schedule), registration fees, and legal representation costs.

Budget for this explicitly. It is not a surprise if you are prepared, but buyers who are not told until the final days of a transaction routinely find it disruptive.

## Month 6+: Closing and Post-Closing

Closing in Costa Rica is conducted by a notary-attorney (notario público) who is a licensed attorney with specific authority to execute public documents. The notary does not represent either party exclusively — they are responsible for the legal correctness of the transaction. Both buyer and seller should have independent legal representation reviewing the closing documents.

Physical presence at closing is not required. Buyers can execute a power of attorney designating a local representative to sign on their behalf. This is common for international buyers and is well-supported by Costa Rican law.

**Post-closing registrations:** After signing, the deed must be registered with the Registro Nacional. This process typically takes 10 to 30 days. During this window, the transfer is not yet public record. Most buyers are not aware that there is a gap between signing and official title transfer — it is normal and expected, but you should know it exists.
